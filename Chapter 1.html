1.1 Introduction of Computer

A computer is an advanced electronic device that accepts raw data as input, stores and processes that data according to a set of programmed instructions, and produces meaningful information as output. Modern society relies heavily on computing infrastructure for everything from global telecommunications to desktop automation.

1.1.1 Definition, Characteristics, and Applications of Computer

At its foundation, a computer functions under the Input-Process-Output (IPO) model. Raw data (facts, numbers, symbols) enters the machine, undergoes algorithmic manipulation in the central processor, and is converted into structured information usable by humans or other automated systems.

Core characteristics:

  • Speed - Computers perform calculations in fractions of a second (nanoseconds and picoseconds). A modern multi-GHz processor executes billions of instructions per second.
  • Accuracy - Computers execute tasks with high precision. Errors generally stem from human programming mistakes or incorrect input data (Garbage In, Garbage Out, GIGO).
  • Diligence - Unlike humans, computers do not suffer from physical tiredness, lack of concentration, or boredom, and can run repetitive tasks continuously without degrading performance.
  • Versatility - A single system can perform vastly different tasks sequentially or simultaneously, from typing a document to rendering 3D graphics or analyzing weather patterns.
  • Storage capacity - Massive volumes of data stored in compact spaces, using volatile primary memory and non-volatile secondary storage for quick retrieval.
  • Automation - Once a program is loaded into memory, instructions execute sequentially without manual intervention at every step.

Key application

  • Education - E-learning platforms, virtual classrooms, digital libraries, academic research tools.
  • Healthcare - Diagnostic imaging (CT, MRI), patient monitoring software, electronic medical records.
  • Banking and Finance - Core Banking Systems, SWIFT transfers, ATMs, algorithmic stock trading.
  • Science and Engineering - CAD software, space exploration simulations, molecular biology analysis.
  • Business and Industry - ERP automation, inventory management, point-of-sale billing, payroll processing.

1.1.2 Evolution of Computer Technology

Electronic computing divides into five generations:

  • First (1940-1956), Vacuum Tubes - fragile, heat-producing glass tubes and magnetic drum memory; massive, power-hungry, programmed in machine language. Examples: ENIAC, UNIVAC-I, EDVAC.
  • Second (1956-1963), Transistors - solid-state semiconductors reduced size and power while raising speed; FORTRAN and COBOL emerged. Examples: IBM 7094, CDC 1604, UNIVAC 1107.
  • Third (1964-1971), Integrated Circuits - hundreds of transistors on one chip cut costs and boosted speed; keyboards, monitors, and early operating systems appeared. Examples: IBM System/360, PDP-8, Honeywell-6000.
  • Fourth (1971-present), Microprocessors - VLSI/ULSI placed millions to billions of transistors on one chip, launching PCs, laptops, and mobile devices. Examples: Intel 8086/Pentium, Apple M-series.
  • Fifth (present and beyond), AI and Quantum - parallel processing, neural networks, machine learning, and quantum mechanics aimed at self-learning and autonomous decision-making. Examples: AI supercomputer clusters, Google Sycamore, IBM Quantum.

1.1.3 Measurement Units of Processing Speed and Storage

The basic unit of digital data is a bit (0 or 1). Storage scales upward in byte multiples:

  • 1 Nibble = 4 bits
  • 1 Byte = 8 bits (one ASCII character)
  • 1 KB = 1,024 Bytes
  • 1 MB = 1,024 KB
  • 1 GB = 1,024 MB
  • 1 TB = 1,024 GB
  • 1 PB = 1,024 TB
  • 1 EB = 1,024 PB

Processing speed was historically measured in MIPS or FLOPS. Modern CPU speed is measured by clock frequency in Hertz - MHz (millions of cycles/sec) and GHz (billions of cycles/sec).

1.1.4 Super, Mainframe, Mini, and Microcomputers

  • Supercomputers - the fastest, largest, most expensive machines, using thousands of parallel processors, measured in petaflops/exaflops. Examples: Frontier, Fugaku, Summit. Used for climate simulation, nuclear physics, weather forecasting, satellite trajectories.
  • Mainframes - high-performance enterprise systems built for massive concurrent transaction throughput and reliability. Examples: IBM zSeries, UNISYS ClearPath. Used for core banking, airline reservations, census processing.
  • Minicomputers - multi-user systems between micro and mainframe scale, serving dozens to hundreds of users. Examples: DEC VAX, AS/400. Used for industrial automation and departmental hosting.
  • Microcomputers - single-user, single-microprocessor systems: PCs, laptops, workstations, tablets. Used for office work, browsing, and multimedia.

1.1.5 Mobile Computing and its Applications

Mobile computing lets users access, transmit, and process data wirelessly without a fixed location, combining mobile hardware, cellular/Wi-Fi/Bluetooth networks, and mobile operating systems.

  • Portability - lightweight hardware carried or worn.
  • Wireless connectivity - continuous radio-frequency communication, no cables.
  • Location awareness - GPS enables location-based services and navigation.

Applications:

  • Mobile banking - transfers, balance checks, bill payments via wallet apps (eSewa, Khalti, Google Pay).
  • Ride-hailing and navigation - Pathao, InDrive, Uber, Google Maps.
  • Mobile commerce - browsing and purchasing via storefront apps (Daraz, Amazon).
  • Remote field operations and telemedicine - on-site data capture, real-time reporting, video consultations.

1.2 Computer System and I/O Devices

A computer system is an integrated assembly of hardware components and software applications designed to perform data processing tasks. Understanding how these sub-systems communicate requires looking at their architectural blueprints and physical hardware components.

1.2.1 Computer Architecture and Organization

  • Architecture - the logical, programmer-visible attributes: instruction set (ISA), data types, registers, addressing modes, memory access. Example: choosing x86 vs. ARM.
  • Organization - the physical implementation: signal timing, bus structures, memory control logic, circuit layout. Example: a 3.2 GHz board with 12 MB L3 cache executing an ARM ISA.

The dominant baseline for general-purpose computing is the Von Neumann Architecture, where instructions and data share a central memory, fetched sequentially over a shared system bus.

1.2.2 Components of a Computer System

  • Input unit - captures external data/commands and converts them to binary signals.
  • Output unit - translates processed data into readable text, graphics, sound, or action.
  • Processing unit (CPU) - executes instructions, performs arithmetic/logic, coordinates data flow.
  • Memory unit (primary) - fast internal storage for active code and data.
  • Storage unit (secondary) - high-capacity non-volatile storage for long-term retention.

1.2.3 Microprocessor

A microprocessor is a single-chip integrated circuit containing a complete CPU.

  • Clock speed - the frequency that synchronizes execution steps, in GHz; higher means more steps per second.
  • Word length - the maximum bits processed per cycle (e.g., 32-bit vs. 64-bit); wider words mean more data per cycle and larger addressable memory.

Core internal components:

  • ALU - performs arithmetic and logical operations.
  • Control Unit - fetches, decodes, and directs instruction execution across the ALU, registers, and peripherals.
  • Registers - small, high-speed storage for temporary values, addresses, and status flags (Program Counter, Accumulator, Instruction Register).

1.2.4 Bus System

  • Data bus - bidirectional; carries the actual data payload between CPU, RAM, and storage.
  • Address bus - unidirectional; CPU selects memory or I/O locations. Its width determines maximum addressable memory.
  • Control bus - carries timing, status, interrupt, and read/write commands from the Control Unit.

1.2.5 Primary Memory

RAM is volatile - read/write access at any address, contents lost without power.

  • SRAM - flip-flop circuits, fast, no refresh needed, expensive; used for CPU cache.
  • DRAM - transistor-capacitor pairs, needs periodic refresh, cheaper and denser; used for main memory (DDR4, DDR5).

ROM is non-volatile - retains firmware such as BIOS/UEFI even without power.

  • PROM - written once with specialized hardware, then fixed.
  • EPROM - erasable via UV light through a quartz window.
  • EEPROM - erasable and rewritable electrically, byte-by-byte.

Cache is a small, fast SRAM buffer near or inside the CPU, holding frequently used instructions and data (L1, L2, L3 tiers). Think of RAM as a bookshelf and cache as the stack of papers on your desk.

Buffer - temporary memory holding data in transit between devices of different speeds, such as a printer buffer.

1.2.6 Secondary Memory

  • Magnetic disk (HDD) - rotating platters, read/write heads change magnetic polarity; high capacity, low cost, mechanically fragile and slower.
  • Flash memory - solid-state NAND storage with no moving parts; powers SSDs with faster speed, lower power draw, better shock resistance.
  • Optical disks - data burned as pits/lands, read by laser.
    • CD - about 700 MB
    • DVD - 4.7 to 8.5 GB
    • Blu-ray - 25 to 100+ GB
  • External storage - portable HDD/SSD enclosures via USB or Thunderbolt.
  • Memory stick / USB drive - thumb-sized flash storage with a USB connector.

1.2.7 Input Devices

  • Keyboard - key presses send scan-codes via a switch matrix. Example: 104-key QWERTY.
  • Mouse - optical sensor tracks 2D movement into cursor motion.
  • Scanner - converts paper documents or photos into digital bitmap images.
  • Light pen - light-sensitive stylus detecting screen position on display phosphors/pixels.
  • OMR - reads reflected light to detect marked areas, as in exam scoring.
  • OCR - reads printed or handwritten text into editable digital text.
  • BCR (barcode reader) - reads reflected laser/LED light off barcodes at checkout.
  • MICR - reads magnetic-ink characters, used in bank check processing.
  • Touch screen - senses finger gestures as input coordinates.
  • Microphone - converts sound waves into electrical audio signals.
  • Digital camera - captures images/video via a CCD or CMOS sensor.

1.2.8 Output Devices

Display monitors (soft-copy):

  • LCD - liquid crystal molecules between polarizing plates, illuminated by a CCFL backlight.
  • LED - LCD variant with LED backlighting for a thinner profile, higher contrast, and lower power use.

Printers (hard-copy):

  • Dot matrix - pins strike an inked ribbon; noisy, low-resolution, supports carbon-copy forms.
  • Inkjet - sprays liquid ink through nozzles for high-resolution color output.
  • Laser - laser and toner on a charged drum, fused by heat; fast and sharp for high-volume printing.

Speakers - convert analog audio signals into sound via a voice coil and cone.

1.2.9 Hardware Interfaces

  • Parallel port (DB-25) - legacy 25-pin interface, 8 bits transferred simultaneously; used for older printers/scanners.
  • Serial port (RS-232 / DB-9) - legacy 9-pin interface, one bit at a time; slower but reliable over distance.
  • USB - standard plug-and-play interface, supplies power and data.
    • Type-A / Type-B - traditional connectors.
    • Type-C - compact, reversible, high-speed, supports video and high-wattage power delivery.
  • HDMI - single-cable digital audio/video from graphics card to display.
  • Expansion slots - motherboard sockets connected to the system bus for add-on cards.
    • PCIe - dominant standard for GPUs, NVMe SSDs, capture cards, and network adapters.
